Every morning I open the same twelve tabs. This is not laziness — it is liturgy.

The same weather widget, the same RSS reader, the same corner of the internet where people talk about music I haven't heard yet. I've been doing this for two years now and I've stopped apologising for it.

We talk about the internet as if it's one thing — a feed, a stream, an algorithm that knows you better than you know yourself. But it's actually a place, or it can be if you treat it that way. A place has regulars. A place has corners you know well and corners you've never been to. A place, if you're lucky, starts to feel like yours.

> Routine is how you make a place yours. The internet has no geography, so you have to build your own.

My routine starts with weather — not because I need to know, but because it orients me in something real before I enter something virtual. Then RSS, which is the closest thing the internet has to a morning newspaper that nobody else edits. Then Radio Garden, usually something from Eastern Europe or West Africa, something that reminds me the internet is bigger than my algorithm thinks it is.

After that it depends on the day. Sometimes Are.na, where I collect images and links I don't fully understand yet. Sometimes the Wikipedia article of the day, which is almost always about something I'd never have looked up on purpose. Sometimes just looking at Lichess openings for ten minutes before I've actually thought about whether I want to play chess.

The twelve tabs are not random. They are the result of years of quiet editing — things added, things removed, things that stayed because they were genuinely useful and not just interesting once. The difference matters: interesting once is most of the internet. Genuinely useful is rare, and worth protecting when you find it.

What I've learned from keeping this routine is that intentionality is more important than discipline. I'm not forcing myself to open these tabs. I want to. They've become the first act of a day I actually want to be in. That's a different thing entirely from productivity hacks or morning routines optimised for output.

The internet rewards people who treat it like a vending machine — put in attention, get out content. The routine I'm describing is something else. It's closer to taking the same walk every morning and noticing what's changed. Sometimes nothing has. Sometimes everything has.

Start somewhere small. One site you visit on purpose, not because an algorithm sent you there. Build from there.
