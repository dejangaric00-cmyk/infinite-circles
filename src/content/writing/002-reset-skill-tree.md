In most games, resetting your character feels like failure. You lose the levels, the gear, the quiet pride of a number going up. I've done it three times now — in Sunflower Land, in the way I think about money, and in the habits I'd built around productivity — and each time, what I kept was worth more than what I lost.

The thing nobody tells you about resetting is that the knowledge doesn't go with it. You know what the mistakes look like now. You know which early choices close off later options. You know which part of the skill tree you actually enjoyed and which part you climbed because the number went up and that felt like progress.

> We accumulate strategies long after the conditions that made them useful have changed.

I reset my Sunflower Land farm after six months because I'd optimised myself into a corner. Every plot planted with the same crop because it had the best yield per hour. Every tool upgraded in the same order because that's what the guides said. It was efficient and completely joyless.

Letting go of it felt like loss. Two weeks later it felt like relief.

The productivity reset was harder because there was no clear moment of failure, no obvious corner I'd painted myself into. Just a slow realisation that the systems I'd built were optimised for a version of my days that no longer existed. The tools had become the task. The maintenance of the system had replaced the thing the system was supposed to support.

Both resets taught me the same thing: you have to be willing to be bad at something again to get good at it differently. The skill tree is always there. The question is whether you're brave enough to put the points somewhere new.
